In Arth, a monster's hoard is spoken for before anyone draws a sword.
Corporate interests claim it and buy it, then sell it off to plunder funds
long before the Heroes' Guild gets round to killing the beast. That's the
premise of [*Orconomics*][orconomics], the first book of J. Zachary Pike's
Dark Profit Saga. I read it in July and went straight on to
[*Son of a Liche*][liche]. I'd tell you to do the same. It's a
well-executed satire of capitalism running amok. It's worth every word.

Every fantasy convention in the book has a financial counterpart. Heroes
need a licence from the guild, which ranks them by points. Firms like
Goldson Baggs, a play on Goldman Sachs, run the funds. Orcs, goblins,
and kobolds have to apply to become Noncombatant Paper Carriers, or NPCs, so
that heroes won't kill and loot them. The pun is a joke about role-playing
games. It's also the paperwork that decides who heroes may kill. From the
start, corporations and governments take an unusual interest in the quest at
the centre of the book. Watch who stands to profit.

Gorm Ingerson has fallen about as far as a hero can and kept his sense of
honour. He's a dwarf who lost his hero's licence and his clan twenty years
before the book opens. He gets by robbing adventurers. He still sticks up for
an undocumented goblin. Word of it gets round. The goblin becomes his squire.
Gorm stays loyal to him, and to the party of misfits around them, which is a
lot of why he's so lovable.

I read it as the 2008 housing crisis. Pike lost his job in 2008 and spent a
lot of time reading and listening to coverage of the financial crisis, he
told [Fantasy Hive][interview]. He doubts many publishers would have looked
at a book about orcs and goblins "lampooning a housing crisis" before it had
an audience. So the reading isn't only mine. I can't tell yet whether that
makes *Orconomics* a satire of one crash or of how money works in general.
