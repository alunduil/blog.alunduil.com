A lighthouse pulls itself out of the ground and learns to walk. That's the
first thing [*Keeper*][keeper] asks you to take on faith. The game spends the
next few hours earning that without once explaining itself.

I played it in June and had a good time. When I try to say what kind, I land
on [*Inside*][inside], Playdead's wordless side-scroller from 2016. I like
that it never narrates and never needs to. The history sits in the world
rather than somebody's mouth, the way the Souls games leave it for you to
find. Games built like that can look thin to a player who wants things said
out loud. They aren't. *Keeper* works that way without the adrenaline.

The refusal is total. No dialogue, no text, no interface, nothing on screen
keeping score. A game can only strip that much away when something else
carries the load. Here that's the beam. The light burns off the Wither, the
purple rot spreading over the island. It brings plants back where the rot has
been. It wakes the mechanisms that open the way. One property, the only
property a lighthouse has ever had, does the work of verb, tutorial, and
explanation at once. Nothing needed saying, because the beam had already said
it.

Where the genre puts pursuit, *Keeper* puts company. Twig, a seabird, reaches
the levers, cranks, and vines a walking lighthouse can't. Most of what the
island asks needs both of them. The same silence that reads as loneliness
elsewhere reads here as partnership.

It escalates anyway. I didn't expect that. Somewhere near the middle the game
changes what you are and how you move. Once you've settled into that, it
changes again. The back half runs faster than the front half. Acceleration
like that usually means something has started chasing you. Here the rising
line is invention. The pace climbs. The threat doesn't. That substitution is
a harder trick than it sounds. It's also why I'd call this a romp rather than
a quiet little game about a lighthouse.

The light itself fought me. The camera doesn't move, which shows the island
only from where the game decides to stand. Aiming a beam is exactly the task
that wants an angle you choose. The framing is mostly generous. The pictures
it picks are better than the ones I'd have chosen. But you're aiming the beam
almost constantly. A camera that won't move makes placing it fiddlier than a
game this sure of its own images ought to need.
