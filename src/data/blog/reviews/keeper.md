A lighthouse pulls itself out of the ground and learns to walk. That's the
first thing [*Keeper*][keeper] asks you to take on faith. The game spends the
next few hours earning that without once explaining itself.

I played it in June and had a good time. When I try to say what kind, I land
on [*Inside*][inside], Playdead's wordless side-scroller from 2016. I like
that it never narrates and never needs to, the history sitting in the world
rather than somebody's mouth, the way the Souls games leave it for you to
find. Games built like that can look thin to a player who wants things said
out loud. They aren't. *Keeper* works that way without the adrenaline.

The refusal is total. No dialogue, no text, no interface, nothing on screen
keeping score. The beam carries it instead, burning off the Wither, bringing
plants back where the purple rot has been, waking the mechanisms that open
the way. One property, the only property a lighthouse has ever had, does the
work of verb, tutorial, and explanation at once.

Where games like this often run on being hunted, *Keeper* runs on company.
Twig, a seabird, reaches the levers, cranks, and vines a walking lighthouse
can't. Most of what the island asks needs both of them. The same silence that
reads as loneliness elsewhere reads here as partnership.

It escalates anyway, which I didn't expect. Somewhere near the middle the
game changes what you are and how you move, then does it again once you've
settled, each turn faster than the last. In most games that acceleration
would mean something had started chasing you. Here what climbs is invention,
not threat. That substitution is a harder trick than it sounds, and the
reason I'd call this a romp rather than a quiet little game about a
lighthouse.

The light itself fought me. Because the camera doesn't move, you see the
island only from wherever the game decides to stand. Aiming a beam is exactly
the task that wants an angle you choose for yourself. The framing is mostly
generous, often better than what I'd have picked. But you aim almost
constantly. A camera that won't move makes placing the beam fiddlier than a
game this sure of its own images ought to need.
