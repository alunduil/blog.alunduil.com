Roke is already there in [*A Wizard of Earthsea*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13642.A_Wizard_of_Earthsea).
Ged arrives at the school and finds an institution older than anyone's
memory of it, the Masters at their disciplines, the Grove standing where it
has always stood. The novel never suggests it was otherwise.
[*Tales from Earthsea*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/13659.Tales_from_Earthsea)
shows it being built, three hundred years earlier, by fugitives, in hiding,
at cost. I read the collection after the novel. It reached back and changed
what the novel had been.

Five stories, set centuries apart, in places with no claim on each other.
Ogion helping his master hold down an earthquake on Gont. A witch's daughter
and a merchant's son on Havnor. A healer walking into a village in Semel
where the cattle are dying. A woman at Iria finding out what she is. Le Guin
doesn't bridge them. The gaps are the point. A novel shows you the world
where its protagonist walks, and Ged's road runs one man across one lifetime
inside an institution at its most settled. You can't see the history of a
place like that from inside its late stage. The reader assembles what no
character in any single story could know.

"The Finder" is where the arc starts. It runs in the dark years, when
wizards served whoever held wealth and power, and it opens on Otter taken
and worked by the mage Gelluk in the mercury mines. The school Ged walks
into as a permanent fact of the world turns out to have been assembled out
of hiding, by people at risk, and largely by women. If you came to Earthsea
for lore, this is the payload.

The history the collection lays out is a narrowing. The school closes over
those three centuries. Women are barred from study and from teaching. Higher
magery is recast as a thing only men do. The Archmage is raised from a
wizard into an office. Barbara Walker's
[*The Woman's Dictionary of Symbols and Sacred Objects*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/408459)
traces that rise across four thousand years of this world's symbols. Le Guin
invents it running the same way—the same reordering of what a symbol means
and of who is permitted to stand near it.
[*The Handmaid's Tale*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/38447) and
[*The Power*](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/29751398) take that
process as their entire subject. Le Guin puts it in the back-history of a
fantasy world. It lands as hard. The school in the first novel admits only
men. The novel doesn't remark on it. This one turns that silence into a
result.

Tolkien answers the same question with appendices. Dated chronologies,
genealogies, languages worked back to their roots, the history settled and
handed over. Le Guin closes this book with thirty-odd pages of much the same
apparatus, on peoples and languages and magic, and then sets it behind five
stories that tell you they may be wrong. She introduces "The Finder" as
something pieced from scraps and fragments, an airy quilt half made of
hearsay and half of guesswork, yet true enough. "For a cloud hangs over the
time when Roke first became the Isle of the Wise," she writes, "and it may be
that the wise men put it there." That last clause is the whole book. The
uncertainty sits inside the world rather than merely withheld from the
reader. It has an author. The mystique and the erasure are one fact.

Both books are reconstructions, which is the part I keep turning over.
Walker reads a lost history out of symbols. Le Guin invents one and says
plainly that hers is guesswork. Neither hands you the thing itself.

I have read two Earthsea books. The mysteries multiply across these five
stories and close almost nothing. That's the reason to keep going rather
than a complaint, though whether the rest of the series pays them off is
something I'm taking on faith. All five stories were good. Only one of them
stayed. Since June I've kept "The Finder" whole in my head while the others
have gone soft, and I can't tell whether that's the shape of the book or
just the one that happened to catch me.
