# The Invincible (game) — review spine

> **Spoilers.** Discovery beats for *The Invincible* (game, 2023) and Lem's
> 1964 novel below. This is a working note, not the published post — the
> post keeps the spoiler fence; this drawer doesn't.

**Object:** video game — Starward Industries / 11 bit Studios, 6 November
2023; first-person, ~6.5 hours, branching endings.
**Issue:** #243, narrowed (see below). **Slug:** `the-invincible`.
**Working title:** Whichever Comes Second.

**Scope note:** #243 was filed as a paired review — *The Invincible* (game)
and *Memoirs of a Space Traveler* — on the premise of one author reached
through two media in a single week. The pairing was a fact about the
calendar, not a claim: a 1957 comic Tichy collection has nothing binding it
to a 1964 hard-SF first-contact novel and its 2023 adaptation except the
byline. Narrowed to the game, measured against the novel it adapts —
the comparison a reviewer who hasn't read the 1964 book cannot make.
*Memoirs* gets its own issue if a take surfaces.

**Thesis:** *The Invincible* is worth playing, and the strangeness of
Regis III still lands even for a reader who already knows what's down
there — the harder trick, and the one it pulls off. What it can't do is
coexist with its source. It isn't a retelling: different protagonist,
different crew, a separate story on the same planet, so there's no
compensating pleasure of a familiar story told a new way. Both works spend
the same discovery, and whichever you come to second is diminished. No
order fixes that; you pick which one to spend at full strength. The walk
through the metallic structures also drags — not enough to ruin the game,
but the long build never delivers the visceral tension the novel gets from
the same material.

**Register constraint:** a game review, not a media-theory essay. The
adaptation question stays at the level of what it was like to play this
having read that — never a thesis about adaptation as a form. Keep the game
load-bearing, ~4:1 argument to summary.

## Supporting points (thematic, not chronological)

1. **Discovery is what the adaptation keeps, and it survives foreknowledge
   better than it has any right to.** The game rebuilds the planet's
   wrongness as something you walk into rather than something explained to
   you, and it stays "reasonably novel even having read the story." A game
   that can make a known planet feel unknown has done the adaptation's real
   job. Evidence: the exploration of the metallic structures — atmosphere
   and accumulating unease doing the work.

2. **It isn't a retelling, so there's no good order — the cost is
   symmetric.** Yasna the astrobiologist in place of Rohan, a different
   crew, a different chain of events. The trade a faithful adaptation
   offers — you know the story, you came for the telling — isn't on the
   table. Both works are still spending the same discovery, and only one of
   them can spend it first. Whichever comes second is the one that pays.
   Rendered spoiler-free: the *shape* of the overlap, never what's found.

3. **The build drags, and the payoff doesn't reach the page.** Same
   sequence as point 1, opposite verdict: the metallic structures go on
   long enough to feel like a drag, and never deliver the visceral tension
   the novel gets from the same material. Short of ruining it — the game is
   good — but the medium trades the book's dread for atmosphere and the
   trade isn't even.

## Honest tail

- The symmetry claim is half-tested. He came to these in one order only,
  novel first, so "whichever comes second is diminished" is firm about the
  path he walked and inference about the one he didn't. Worth admitting
  plainly; it's the honest close, not a hedge.
- Good and wanting. The game left him wanting more, which is the review in
  miniature: enough to recommend, not enough to match its source.

## Spoiler approach

The thesis is *about* spoiling, which makes the fence load-bearing rather
than decorative — a review arguing that foreknowledge diminishes the game
cannot itself supply the foreknowledge.

- **Name the strangeness, never its content.** "What's on Regis III" stays
  unspecified in the post. No necrosphere, no mechanism, no ending.
- **The overlap is described structurally.** That the two works spend the
  same discovery is a claim about their relationship, not a reveal of what
  the discovery is.
- **The test:** could a reader who then plays the game still be surprised?
  The frontmatter description is strictest — it must not even name the
  category of thing found.

## Resolved

- **Long build:** a drag, short of ruining the game. Point 3 states it as a
  real criticism, sized so it doesn't swallow the recommendation.
- **No right order.** An earlier draft of this spine argued play-the-game-
  first, reading asymmetry into "either will spoil the other." The author
  corrected it: the cost runs both ways, so the claim is that you choose
  which work to spend at full strength, not that one sequence is correct.
- **The *Inside*/Souls lens is out.** It came from a prior session's memory
  rather than anything the author said here, and he didn't recognise it on
  this work. The order argument stands on its own without it.

## Open questions for the author

1. **Title.** "Whichever Comes Second" is the working pick — noun phrase,
   carries the symmetric claim, matches *The Sicilian*'s "The Man in the
   Middle." Alternatives: "No Good Order," or "You Only Get It Once"
   (the discovery is spent once, by whichever work reaches you first).
2. **Does the recommendation survive the drag in your own read?** The spine
   has it as yes — good game, real flaw. If the drag looms larger than that
   for you, point 3 grows and the thesis softens toward conditional.
