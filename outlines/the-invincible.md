# The Invincible (game) — review spine

> **Spoilers.** Discovery beats for *The Invincible* (game, 2023) and Lem's
> 1964 novel below. This is a working note, not the published post — the
> post keeps the spoiler fence; this drawer doesn't.

**Object:** video game — Starward Industries / 11 bit Studios, 6 November
2023; first-person, ~6.5 hours, branching endings.
**Issue:** #243, narrowed (see below). **Slug:** `the-invincible`.
**Working title:** The Wrong Order.

**Scope note:** #243 was filed as a paired review — *The Invincible* (game)
and *Memoirs of a Space Traveler* — on the premise of one author reached
through two media in a single week. The pairing was a fact about the
calendar, not a claim: a 1957 comic Tichy collection has nothing binding it
to a 1964 hard-SF first-contact novel and its 2023 adaptation except the
byline. Narrowed to the game, measured against the novel it adapts —
the comparison a reviewer who hasn't read the 1964 book cannot make.
*Memoirs* gets its own issue if a take surfaces.

**Thesis:** Play *The Invincible*, but play it before you read Lem. The
adaptation's achievement is making Regis III strange again, and the
strangeness lands even for a reader who already knows what's down there —
which is the harder trick. But it isn't a retelling. Different protagonist,
different crew, a separate story on the same planet, so there's no
compensating pleasure of a familiar story told a new way. What you get is
two independent visits to one world, each blunting the other's discovery.
The novel blunts the game worse. Hence the order.

**Register constraint:** a game review, not a media-theory essay. The
adaptation question stays at the level of what it was like to play this
having read that — never a thesis about adaptation as a form. Keep the game
load-bearing, ~4:1 argument to summary.

**Lens (implicit, per [[user_game_story_taste]]):** the author values story
that sits in the world waiting to be found — *Inside*, the Souls games —
over story narrated at him; he left Destiny when the voice acting arrived
and took the mystique with it. That's why the order matters more here than
generic spoiler etiquette would suggest: discovery *is* the payload, so
front-loading it with the novel damages the specific thing he plays for.
See open question 2 on whether this surfaces explicitly.

## Supporting points (thematic, not chronological)

1. **Discovery is what the adaptation keeps, and it's the thing worth
   having.** The game rebuilds the planet's wrongness as something you walk
   into rather than something explained to you. Evidence: the long
   exploration of the metallic structures — atmosphere and accumulating
   unease doing the work. The claim: it's "reasonably novel even having read
   the story," and a game that can make a known planet feel unknown has done
   the adaptation's real job.

2. **It isn't a retelling, so the order is load-bearing.** Yasna the
   astrobiologist in place of Rohan, a different crew, a different chain of
   events. The trade a faithful adaptation offers — you know the story, you
   came for the telling — isn't on the table. Both works are still spending
   the same discovery, and only one of them can spend it first. Rendered
   spoiler-free: the *shape* of the overlap, never what's found.

3. **The payoff doesn't reach the page.** Same sequence, opposite verdict:
   the metallic structures build tension for a long stretch and never
   deliver the visceral tension the novel gets from the same material. Good,
   not great. The medium trades the book's dread for atmosphere, and the
   trade isn't even.

## Honest tail

- The recommendation is one the author can't test. He read the novel first,
  so "it would be better if I had played the game without the novel" is a
  counterfactual — a strong intuition about an experience he didn't have.
  Worth admitting plainly; it's the honest close, not a hedge.
- Good and wanting. The game left him wanting more, which is the review in
  miniature: enough to recommend, not enough to match its source.

## Spoiler approach

The thesis is *about* spoiling, which makes the fence load-bearing rather
than decorative — a review arguing that foreknowledge damages the game
cannot itself supply the foreknowledge.

- **Name the strangeness, never its content.** "What's on Regis III" stays
  unspecified in the post. No necrosphere, no mechanism, no ending.
- **The overlap is described structurally.** That the two works spend the
  same discovery is a claim about their relationship, not a reveal of what
  the discovery is.
- **The test:** could a reader who then plays the game still be surprised?
  The frontmatter description is strictest — it must not even name the
  category of thing found.

## Open questions for the author

1. **Is the long build a virtue or a drag?** "Built the tension for a long
   time and didn't have the same visceral tension" reads to me as: patient
   setup, weaker payoff than the book. But it could equally mean the build
   overstayed. These are different sentences in the post.
2. **Does the *Inside*/Souls lens surface explicitly?** Naming it makes the
   order argument land harder — it explains *why* discovery is the thing
   being protected — but it also imports a niche position into a short
   review. My inclination: one clause, not a paragraph.
3. **Title.** "The Wrong Order" is the working pick — noun phrase, carries
   the claim, matches *The Sicilian*'s "The Man in the Middle." Alternative:
   "Play It Before You Read It," which states the thesis flat but reads more
   like a headline than the corpus does.
